Temperature & Light Sensors

Thermocouples, RTDs, and thermistors all turn heat into an electrical signal, but with very different curves. Photoelectric sensors instead detect objects using light in three different geometries.

Thermocouple output rises roughly linearly in millivolts (type K ≈ 41µV/°C). RTD resistance rises gently and very linearly (Pt100 ≈ 0.385Ω/°C). NTC thermistor resistance instead drops sharply and non-linearly as it heats up — fast response, but only accurate over a narrow range.

Knowledge Notes

  • Thermocouples need cold junction compensation (CJC): since the meter/module's own terminals form a second junction, the module measures its own terminal temperature (usually via a small built-in sensor) and adds it back to get the true reading.
  • RTD wiring: 2-wire is cheapest but lead resistance adds directly to the reading; 3-wire (the industrial standard) cancels lead resistance by measuring it on a matched extra wire; 4-wire is lab-grade, eliminating lead resistance entirely.
  • Thermocouple types trade off range and cost: K (general purpose, -200 to 1260°C), J (cheaper, narrower range, degrades in oxidizing atmospheres above 550°C), T (accurate at cryogenic/sub-zero temperatures).
  • Photoelectric sensors have a "light-on" or "dark-on" output mode setting — choose based on whether the output should activate when the beam is received or when it's blocked.
  • "Excess gain" is a photoelectric spec describing margin against dust, fog, or a dirty lens — higher excess gain means more reliable operation in harsh environments.

3-Wire RTD Wiring & a Bang-Bang Heater Ladder

|--[ D10 < K1800 ]------------------------------( Y2 )--|  // D10: scaled temp x10 (180.0C) -> heater ON
|--[ D10 >= K2000 ]-----------------------(RST Y2 )--|  // above 200.0C -> heater OFF (hysteresis band)
Applied example: a simple "bang-bang" oven controller turns the heater relay Y2 ON below 180.0°C and OFF at or above 200.0°C — the 20°C gap (hysteresis) stops the relay from chattering on/off right at one exact setpoint. The same photoelectric through-beam logic from this unit is also used to count objects passing on a conveyor, incrementing a counter each time the beam breaks.
